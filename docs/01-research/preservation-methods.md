# Preservation Methods for Broccoli Microgreens

**Phase:** 01 Research
**Status:** Complete
**Last Updated:** 2026-02-17

---

## Summary

Five preservation methods are evaluated: refrigeration (fresh), **raw home freezing**, freeze-drying, low-temperature dehydration, and high-temperature dehydration. **Raw home freezing is a surprising standout** — it is free (uses a standard freezer), preserves glucoraphanin fully, and actually *increases* myrosinase activity and sulforaphane yield (up to 3× vs. unfrozen fresh), provided the greens were never blanched and are blended directly from frozen. **Refrigeration** remains best for daily fresh consumption with no processing. **Freeze-drying is the gold standard** for long shelf life but requires expensive equipment. **Low-temperature dehydration** is a budget alternative with some nutrient loss. The device design should support fresh harvest (primary) and raw-freezing batches (practical backup).

---

## Critical Consideration: Myrosinase and Sulforaphane Activation

**This is the most important factor** when considering preservation of broccoli microgreens. The key enzyme myrosinase, which converts glucoraphanin (GRN) into sulforaphane (SFN), is:

- **Heat-sensitive:** Destroyed above ~60–70°C. Any preservation method using temperatures above this threshold will eliminate myrosinase activity.
- **Required for SFN activation:** If myrosinase is destroyed, the GRN in the preserved greens cannot be converted to SFN without an external enzyme source (e.g., mustard seed powder).
- **Partially preserved by freeze-drying:** Freeze-drying preserves myrosinase better than heat methods, but some activity loss still occurs.

**The practical implication:** Preserved microgreens (especially powders or capsules) may require the user to add a pinch of mustard seed powder or another myrosinase source to activate the sulforaphane potential — unless the product was pre-converted before drying.

## Method 1: Fresh Refrigeration (Primary Recommended Method)

### Process
Harvest, allow greens to dry completely (no surface moisture), store unwashed in an airtight container lined with paper towel in the refrigerator.

### Performance

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Shelf life | 5–7 days |
| Sulforaphane retention | Near 100% (myrosinase intact) |
| Vitamin C retention | Near 100% short-term, degrades faster unwashed |
| Vitamin E retention | Near 100% |
| Equipment required | Refrigerator only |
| Ongoing cost | None |
| Volume ratio | 1:1 (no concentration) |

### Pros
- Maximum myrosinase activity and sulforaphane bioavailability
- No processing equipment required
- No nutrient losses from heat or drying
- Simplest integration with the growing device

### Cons
- Short shelf life (5–7 days)
- Requires daily or near-daily consumption
- Cannot batch-process large harvests for extended storage

### Best for
A device designed around daily fresh consumption with a rotating tray system. The user harvests ~30g every day or every few days from a staggered rotation. **This is the primary mode for this project.**

## Method 2: Raw Home Freezing — Surprising Enhancement of Sulforaphane

### Process
Harvest microgreens, allow to dry slightly, then freeze **raw and unblanched** in airtight bags or containers at −20°C (standard home freezer). When consuming, **blend directly from frozen — do not thaw first**.

### The Science (Counterintuitive)
Most people assume freezing destroys nutrients. For broccoli microgreens specifically, the opposite is true for sulforaphane — under the right conditions:

1. **Ice crystals rupture cell walls**, bringing myrosinase and glucoraphanin into contact — the same reaction triggered by chewing or blending.
2. **Myrosinase is NOT destroyed by freezing** (unlike heat above 60°C). Freezing actually increases measurable myrosinase activity.
3. **Result:** Freezing at −20°C for 24h increased sulforaphane yield by up to **3.1×** compared to unfrozen controls in published research.
4. **Glucoraphanin is fully preserved** — no significant degradation during freezing.

**The critical caveat — The Thawing Problem:**
When frozen greens thaw, the cell-damage liquid (containing active myrosinase and formed sulforaphane) drains out. If you:
- **Pour off or discard any liquid when thawing → you lose most of the sulforaphane**
- **Thaw slowly at room temperature → sulforaphane forms then degrades before you consume it**

**Solution:** Blend directly from frozen. The Jed Fahey recommendation (leading sulforaphane researcher, Johns Hopkins): *"throw it in the blender right away."* This captures 100% of the enzymatic activity and forms sulforaphane in the blender right before consumption.

**Critical distinction from commercial frozen broccoli:** Commercial frozen broccoli is **blanched** (hot water/steam) before freezing to prevent discoloration — this destroys myrosinase completely and leaves broccoli with near-zero sulforaphane potential. Home freezing of **unblanched, raw** microgreens is a completely different situation.

### Performance

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Shelf life | 3–6 months (raw, unblanched, airtight) |
| Glucoraphanin retention | ~100% |
| Myrosinase activity | **Increased** (+17–117% depending on temp and cultivar) |
| Sulforaphane yield (if blended from frozen) | **Up to 3.1× higher than fresh unfrozen** |
| Vitamin C retention | ~85–94% (6–15% loss; comparable to refrigeration) |
| Vitamin E retention | ~90–95% |
| Equipment required | Standard kitchen freezer |
| Ongoing cost | $0 |
| Volume ratio | 1:1 (no concentration) |

### Recommended Protocol
1. Harvest microgreens; pat dry with paper towel (minimize surface moisture)
2. Portion into daily-dose bags (30g per bag) — seal immediately
3. Freeze flat at −20°C for at least 24 hours before use
4. **When consuming: empty frozen bag directly into blender. Do not thaw. Blend immediately with fruit and liquid.**
5. Drink within 20 minutes of blending

### Pros
- **Free** — every kitchen has a freezer
- Sulforaphane yield equal to or better than fresh
- Enables batch processing: harvest a full tray (75–165g), portion into daily bags, freeze for 3–6 months
- Vitamins C and E well-preserved (~85–95%)
- Simplifies daily routine: no daily harvesting, just grab a frozen bag and blend

### Cons
- Only practical when blended (not for eating raw/whole from frozen)
- Requires airtight portioned bags to prevent freezer burn
- 3–6 month shelf life (vs. 12+ months for freeze-drying)
- Studies on freezing are primarily on broccoli sprouts; mechanism is the same for microgreens but specific yield numbers may vary slightly

### Best for
**Batch processing surplus harvests.** When a tray yields 100–165g and the user only needs 30g immediately, the remainder can be portioned and frozen. This is an excellent complement to the primary fresh consumption method.

## Method 3: Freeze-Drying (Best for Long-Term Preservation)

### Process
Freeze microgreens at very low temperature (typically −40°C), then apply a vacuum causing the frozen water to sublimate directly from ice to vapor (bypassing the liquid phase). Result: a lightweight, porous, shelf-stable powder or whole-leaf product.

### Performance

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Shelf life | 12+ months (properly stored in airtight, dark, cool conditions) |
| Sulforaphane precursor retention | Very high — GRN well-preserved |
| Myrosinase retention | Partial — some activity preserved; less than fresh |
| Vitamin C retention | ~90–95% |
| Vitamin E retention | ~90–95% |
| Mineral retention | Near 100% |
| Fresh-weight equivalent | 1g freeze-dried ≈ 20g fresh |
| Equipment required | Freeze dryer (~$2,000–$5,000 home unit) |
| Ongoing cost | Electricity; vacuum pump maintenance |

### Volume Concentration
The **20:1 concentration ratio** (1g freeze-dried = 20g fresh) is significant:
- 30g daily fresh dose → **1.5g freeze-dried powder**
- 60g daily fresh dose → **3.0g freeze-dried powder**
- This makes capsule delivery practical (see [consumption-methods.md](consumption-methods.md))

### Myrosinase Caveat
Freeze-drying preserves GRN very well but partially denatures myrosinase. To maximize sulforaphane when using freeze-dried powder:
1. Add a pinch of mustard seed powder (provides exogenous myrosinase) when consuming
2. Hydrate the powder briefly (15–20 min) before consuming to allow residual myrosinase to react
3. Or use a powder that was pre-converted (GRN→SFN) before drying — but SFN itself is less stable than GRN

### Pros
- Excellent shelf life (1+ year)
- Very high nutrient retention vs. any heat-based method
- Concentrated (easy to store and consume)
- Enables capsule packaging
- Commercial products exist (e.g., BeMicro, Revity Farms, Silicon Valley Microgreens)

### Cons
- Expensive equipment ($2,000–$5,000 for home freeze dryer)
- Slow process (24–48 hours per batch)
- Partial myrosinase loss (manageable with mustard seed trick)
- Not practical as the primary daily delivery method for a home device

## Method 3: Low-Temperature Dehydration (Practical Home Option)

### Process
Dry microgreens in a food dehydrator or oven at ≤40°C (104°F) until completely dry. Can be ground into powder after drying.

### Performance

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Shelf life | Several months (airtight container) |
| Sulforaphane precursor retention | Moderate — GRN partially preserved |
| Myrosinase retention | Reduced but not zero at ≤40°C |
| Vitamin C retention | ~60–70% (heat-sensitive) |
| Vitamin E retention | ~70–80% |
| Mineral retention | Near 100% |
| Overall nutrient retention vs. fresh | ~60–80% depending on compound |
| Equipment required | Food dehydrator ($50–$200) |
| Ongoing cost | Electricity |

### Critical Temperature Note
- ≤40°C: Meaningful myrosinase activity preserved; acceptable nutrient retention
- 40–60°C: Myrosinase partially active; sulforaphane conversion possible but reduced
- >60°C: Myrosinase completely destroyed; sulforaphane conversion requires external enzyme
- >70°C: Significant vitamin C and B-vitamin loss

### Pros
- Inexpensive equipment
- Practical for home use
- Enables longer storage than refrigeration
- Can be incorporated into the device design (passive or active low-temp drying chamber)

### Cons
- 30–40% nutrient loss vs. fresh (especially heat-sensitive vitamins)
- Slower than freeze-drying in terms of product quality
- Requires temperature control to stay ≤40°C

---

## Method 4: High-Temperature Dehydration (Not Recommended for SFN)

Dehydration above 60°C (common food dehydrator default, or oven drying) destroys myrosinase entirely and causes significant losses of vitamin C (up to 70%), B vitamins, and folate. **This method should not be used if sulforaphane delivery is the goal.** Acceptable only if the powder will be consumed with an external myrosinase source (mustard seed powder).

## Selected Method for This Device

**Primary: Fresh refrigeration** — The device grows microgreens on a staggered rotation. Harvested greens are consumed fresh within 5–7 days. This maximizes sulforaphane bioavailability, requires no additional equipment, and is operationally simple.

**Recommended secondary: Raw home freezing** — When a tray produces more than the immediate 5–7 day supply, the surplus is portioned (30g per bag) and frozen **unblanched** at −20°C. Consumed by blending directly from frozen. This is **free, simple, and preserves or enhances sulforaphane yield**. It is a significantly better backup method than dehydration and costs nothing. This finding changes the operational model: the device can produce larger harvests less frequently (e.g., one large tray every 10 days), with the daily surplus frozen for later use.

**Optional tertiary: Low-temperature dehydration (≤40°C)** — For users who want a shelf-stable powder for travel or smoothie convenience without a blender. Requires a food dehydrator (~$50–$150). Nutrient loss is ~30–40% vs. fresh.

**Not prioritized: Freeze-drying** — The $2,000–$5,000 equipment cost is out of scope. Commercial freeze-dried broccoli microgreen powder is available separately if the capsule format is desired.
